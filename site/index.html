Explainable heart-disease screening

Fewer tests for those who don't need them. More care for those who do.

CardioStage starts with a simple check-up and only suggests the next, more invasive test when it's likely to change the answer. Every estimate comes with a clear explanation.

How it works

One step at a time, the way a careful doctor would.

Each stage asks only for what's needed. If the answer is already clear, you stop there.

  1. Stage 1

    A simple check-up

    Age, symptoms, blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar. Things any clinic can measure in one visit.

    Of 243 people: 26 reassured, 33 referred, 184 go on to one more test.

    • Low risk: reassured
    • High risk: referred
    • Unsure: one more test
  2. Stage 2

    A treadmill test

    Watching the heart under gentle exercise: how fast it can beat, and how its rhythm changes.

  3. Stage 3

    Detailed imaging

    An X-ray of the arteries and a heart scan, kept for the people who genuinely need them.

Results

The same safety, with far fewer invasive tests.

Both approaches are set to catch 97% of sick patients. CardioStage gets there while sparing about four in ten people the dye X-ray and the nuclear scan.

Honest by design

A number you can understand, and trust.

It means what it says

When CardioStage says 30%, about 30 in 100 such patients had heart disease. Typically within 3–5 points.

It explains itself

Every estimate shows which measurements raised or lowered it, in plain language.

It's open about its limits

It flags patients unlike anyone it learned from, and says plainly where it can go wrong.

Tested beyond home

Version 1: trained in Cleveland, checked in three other hospitals.

Ranking quality (ROC-AUC, 0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect) with 95% intervals. Hungary looks like Cleveland; VA Long Beach is harder; Switzerland had only 8 healthy patients, so we honestly can't tell.

Version 2

Then we tested it on modern patients, and learned from them.

On 303 patients from Iran (2010s), version 1 missed about a third of sick patients: it had learned from 1980s Cleveland that typical angina means low risk. Version 2's check-up model learns from all five hospitals. Same sealed-test patients for both versions below.

  • Typical angina, then and now: in Iran, 97% of these patients were sick. Version 1 estimated 28%, version 2 81%: much better, still somewhat low, so the app keeps its warning.
  • An honest weak spot: at VA Long Beach, version 2 ranks its 40 test patients worse than version 1 did. Version 1 was unusually lucky there; on hospitals neither version saw, both score about 0.70 at VA.
  • A flaw we caught and fixed: pooling first taught the model that lower cholesterol means more risk, because Iran's cholesterol is estimated, in a modern population. That value is now treated as not measured.
  • Two looks at the test: these numbers are a second look after that fix, so they're slightly optimistic. The first, unbiased look at the first version 2 was AUC 0.84 with 1 sick patient wrongly reassured.
Why not just ask a chatbot?

We asked two, on the same patients.

The same 245 sealed-test patients and the same six check-up facts went to free ChatGPT and paid Claude (Opus 5.5), 14–15 patients per fresh chat, one try each. The prompt and every measure were published before any patient was sent.

  • Ranking: paid Claude ranked patients about as well as CardioStage (no clear difference). Free ChatGPT was clearly worse.
  • Honest percentages: both chatbots guessed too low across the board, about 50% on average where 59% were sick. CardioStage's calibration error was 0.040, the chatbots' about 0.11.
  • Safety: CardioStage reassured 11% of patients and none were sick. Claude reassured fewer and included 1 sick patient, ChatGPT 3. Small numbers, but a chatbot's percentages come with no tested cut-off at all.
  • Where the chatbots win: on typical angina, CardioStage's known weak spot, both chatbots told sick from healthy better. Textbook knowledge helps exactly where 1980s data misled.
  • Read with care: free ChatGPT vs paid Claude, one try each, several patients per message. Claude did slightly better on Cleveland, a famous public dataset, than on Iran's: possibly memorised, not proven.
What it can't do

Limitations, stated plainly.

  • Version 1 missed about a third of sick patients in a modern test (Iran). Version 2 learns from five hospitals and fixes most of that, but still somewhat underestimates "typical angina" (the app warns about it) and did worse than version 1 at VA Long Beach.
  • Small, mostly old data: the check-up stage learned from 976 patients at five hospitals (1980s–2010s), the later stages from 243 patients at one US hospital in the 1980s.
  • Everyone in the data was already referred for heart tests, so its patterns don't apply to the general public.
  • Its decision thresholds were tuned in Cleveland; any new setting would need to check them again.
  • The imaging stage couldn't be checked at other hospitals, which lacked that data.
  • A woman with atypical chest pain was reassured too early in testing, mirroring how heart disease in women is often under-recognised.
Why I built this

I built CardioStage as my first real dive into medicine, and I chose the heart because it demands more attention to detail than almost anything else. It was also the first time I'd challenged myself with a project where the people behind the data are real, and where every percentage truly matters. What surprised me most was how many factors can shift a heart-risk estimate, far more than I expected. This isn't a medical-grade tool, and I'm still learning both machine learning and medicine, but I gave it everything I had, and I'll keep improving it as I discover new methods and better ways to evaluate it.

Harsh Gupta · builder of CardioStage
